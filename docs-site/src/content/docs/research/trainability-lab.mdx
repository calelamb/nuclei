The **Trainability Lab** is Research mode's microscope for parameterized
circuits. Point it at the circuit in your editor and it answers one question
with evidence: *will gradient-based training of this ansatz get stuck on a
barren plateau, and could a classical method simulate it anyway?* Open it from
the **Trainability Lab** item in the Research rail. It needs the desktop
kernel; the web build shows a notice instead.

<Aside type="note">
Run the analyses with the **Run analyses** button or <kbd>⌘⇧↵</kbd>
(<kbd>Ctrl+Shift+Enter</kbd> on Windows/Linux). <kbd>⌘↵</kbd> keeps its global
meaning, running your code on the simulator.
</Aside>

## What you point it at

The Lab runs the code in your editor once (the same trust as <kbd>⌘↵</kbd>:
it is your own code), finds the parameterized Qiskit or Cirq circuit, and
converts it to an internal parameterized circuit IR. Unbound symbols must be
parameters of the circuit, not free variables. If the code reads
`params.get("n_qubits")`, the Lab offers that name for the scaling study.

The **observable** is resolved in this order: the field in the target bar, a
module-level `nuclei_observable` in your code (a string, a Qiskit
`SparsePauliOp` or a Cirq `PauliSum`), then `preset:local_z`. Presets are
`preset:local_z` (mean of Z on each qubit), `preset:global_z` (Z on every
qubit), `preset:zz_chain` and `preset:tfim`. A malformed observable is
reported with the column of the problem.

## The analyses

| Analysis | What it measures | Main assumptions |
|----------|------------------|------------------|
| **Gradients** | The variance of each parameter's partial derivative of the cost, over random parameter draws, plus the mean over parameters | Parameters drawn uniformly on [0, 2π) (`uniform`) or from a narrow Gaussian near zero (`small`); initial state \|0…0⟩ |
| **Scaling** | How that variance changes with qubit count n (and optionally layer count): a fit of Var ∝ 2^(−βn) against a power law in n | Your code builds a comparable circuit for each n through `params`; at least 4 distinct n for a verdict |
| **Lie algebra** | The dimension of the dynamical Lie algebra 𝔤 generated by the circuit's gates, its classification, and the Lie-algebraic variance prediction | Deep-circuit regime (the circuit approximates a 2-design over e^𝔤); 𝔤 treated as simple |
| **Expressibility** | KL divergence between the circuit's distribution of state overlaps and the Haar distribution | Random draws of parameter pairs; limited by pair count and bin count |
| **Entanglement** | Meyer-Wallach entangling capability averaged over random parameters | Statevector simulation, so at most 20 qubits |
| **Landscape** | A 2-D slice of the cost along two random orthonormal directions through a random center | A slice shows local structure only |

Statevector-based analyses (sampling gradients, expressibility, entanglement,
landscape) are capped at 20 qubits by the kernel, and at 14 by the default UI
settings. The exact engine below goes to 128.

## Gradient variance: three kinds of interval

Every variance in the Lab is shown with its method and an interval. They are
not interchangeable:

- **Bootstrap interval (sampling).** The kernel draws `samples` parameter
  vectors (16 to 4096, default 200), computes exact gradients for each with a
  compiled batched simulator, and reports the sample variance with a 95%
  percentile bootstrap interval (1000 seeded resamples). It says how well
  *this finite sample* pins down the variance. It is statistical, and it
  shrinks as you add samples.
- **PPE exact.** The **Pauli Propagation Engine** walks the observable
  backwards through the circuit as a sum of Pauli strings and computes the
  variance analytically. When no term had to be discarded, the answer is
  exact: the interval has zero width, there is no sampling noise, and it works
  up to 128 qubits. It is labelled `pauli_propagation_exact`.
- **PPE bounded.** When the frontier of Pauli strings outgrows the budget
  (`max_frontier`, or a time budget), the engine discards the smallest terms
  and tracks the total weight it threw away. The result is still *rigorous*:
  the interval is a truncation bound that provably contains the true value,
  but it can be loose. It is labelled `pauli_propagation_bounded` and the UI
  calls the interval a truncation interval, not a confidence interval.
  *Revised after review:* when the bound is too loose to mean anything
  (the sweep timed out, the lower end is 0, or the upper end is more than twice
  the lower), the Lab shows **no number**: the variance is `null` and only the
  interval is shown. It never reports the middle of the interval.

`method: auto` (the default) uses the engine when it applies and its bound is
tight, and falls back to sampling otherwise. You can force `sampling` or `ppe`.
The engine applies when every rotation is a single-Pauli rotation with its own
independent parameter (no shared parameters, no nonzero offset on a parameter)
and every fixed gate is Clifford. Circuits outside that class use sampling, and
the Lab tells you why.

## Verdict rules

**Scaling verdict.** The fit is weighted least squares on log₂(variance), with
weights from the interval widths. The exponential model has rate β. Models are
compared by AIC.

| Verdict | When |
|---------|------|
| **Barren plateau** | The exponential model is preferred by ΔAIC ≥ 2 **and** the lower end of β's 95% interval is above 0.1 |
| **Polynomial** | β's 95% interval lies entirely at or below 0.1 (its **upper** end is ≤ 0.1), **or** the power-law model is preferred by ΔAIC ≥ 2 |
| **Inconclusive** | Anything else. Always when fewer than 4 distinct qubit counts were fitted, when the largest n is less than twice the smallest, and when any point has a zero or null variance or an uninformative interval (lower end 0, or upper end more than twice the lower) |

*Revised after review.* The earlier rule called a fit polynomial whenever β's
interval merely *included* values at or below 0.1, so a wide interval became a
positive verdict. On synthetic data over n = 4 to 7 that called `n⁻¹` a barren
plateau 22% of the time and `2^(−0.25n)` polynomial 22.5% of the time. Now a
narrow range is `inconclusive`, and `polynomial` needs either a small upper
bound or a model-selection win.

*Inconclusive is a real answer.* The Lab never rounds an unclear fit up to a
verdict. β's interval is a parametric bootstrap: each point's log-variance is
redrawn from its own interval and the slope refitted, so the point intervals
(sampling or Pauli-propagation truncation) carry into β.

**Barren-plateau risk (the verdict rail).** The rail combines the evidence, and
lists every item so you can see why. Measured scaling outranks theory:

1. A `barren_plateau` scaling verdict gives **high**; `polynomial` gives **low**
   (raised to **medium** if a lint warning about a global cost or an
   exponentially large algebra also fired).
2. With no usable scaling verdict, an exponentially large Lie algebra gives
   **high** when the closure completed and **medium** when it was truncated; a
   small algebra gives **low**.
3. With neither, a TRN001 or TRN002 lint warning gives **medium**.
4. Otherwise the risk is **unknown**. The Lab does not guess.

**Simulability.** If the algebra's dimension is polynomial (at most 4n²), the
Lab notes that expectations may be classically simulable with 𝔤-sim (Goh et
al. 2023) when the observable or the state lies in 𝔤. Whether a circuit
resists the Pauli-propagation estimator is not a statement about classical
hardness: *estimability is resistance to this estimator under this budget; it
is not a proof of classical simulability.*

## Lie algebra and the variance prediction

The closure is computed on the *effective generators*: fixed gates are pushed
to the end of the circuit, so each rotation generator is conjugated by the
fixed gates before it. With only Clifford fixed gates this is exact Pauli
conjugation and scales to about 20 qubits; non-Clifford fixed gates use a dense
fallback for at most 6 qubits and are otherwise `unsupported`. If the closure
hits the dimension cap (4096 by default) or the time budget (10 s) the status
is `truncated` and only a lower bound on the dimension is shown.

**Classification is by exact dimension match** to su(2ⁿ), so(2ⁿ), sp(2ⁿ⁻¹) and
the free-fermion algebra so(2n). A match is *not* an isomorphism proof, and the
note says so. Anything else is reported as `other`.

The prediction Var = P_𝔤(ρ) P_𝔤(O) / dim 𝔤 (Ragone et al., 2024) is shown
next to the empirical measurement, with its stated assumptions, and is marked
applicable only when the observable or the initial state lies in 𝔤. When it
does not apply, the Lab says so rather than printing a number.

*Revised after review.* The formula is the sum over the simple ideals of the
algebra, Var = Σⱼ P_𝔤ⱼ(ρ) P_𝔤ⱼ(O) / dim 𝔤ⱼ, after removing the centre
(generators that commute with everything). The single-ideal version is wrong for
an algebra such as su(2) ⊕ su(16) (0.062 predicted, 0.336 measured). It is
computed only where the ideals can be found exactly (Pauli-string generators)
and for the provably simple su(2ⁿ); for other shared-parameter or multi-Pauli
circuits the Lab reports "not applicable". The prediction is for the variance of
the **cost** over parameter space, so it is compared with the measured cost
variance, never with the gradient variance.

**Polynomial dimension is not trainability.** A full su, so or sp of 2ⁿ is the
exponential family even when its dimension is small (so(8) at n = 3): it is
never reported as 𝔤-sim feasible. A genuinely polynomial dimension suggests
𝔤-sim simulability; it does not by itself bound the purities or guarantee
trainability.

## Lint rules

Lint runs as you type with a 0.35 s compute budget and draws markers in the
editor. Rules that need a complete Lie-algebra closure (TRN002, TRN003, TRN006)
are skipped, with a note, if the closure does not finish in time.

| Code | Severity | Fires when | Reference |
|------|----------|------------|-----------|
| TRN001 Global cost function | warning | An observable term acts on at least max(3, ⌈n/2⌉+1) qubits, and n ≥ 4 | [Cerezo et al., Nat. Commun. 12, 1791 (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-21728-w) |
| TRN002 Exponentially large Lie algebra | warning | The closure is complete and its dimension matches su, so or sp of 2ⁿ, for n ≥ 4 | [Ragone et al., Nat. Commun. 15, 7172 (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-49909-3) |
| TRN003 Polynomial Lie algebra | info | The dimension is at most 4n² and is not a full su/so/sp family: the circuit is likely 𝔤-sim simulable (this does not by itself guarantee trainability) | [Goh et al., arXiv:2308.01432 (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01432) |
| TRN004 Dead parameter | info | A parameterized gate's generator commutes with the observable after conjugation and nothing later changes that: it cannot affect the cost | n/a (a direct computation) |
| TRN005 Redundant consecutive rotations | info | Two consecutive rotations about the same axis on the same qubit with independent parameters | n/a |
| TRN006 Overparameterized circuit | info | The number of parameters is at least the algebra's dimension | [Larocca et al., Nat. Comput. Sci. 3, 542 (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-023-00467-6) |
| TRN007 No free parameters | info | The circuit has none; the Lab is inactive (reported once in the summary, never as a marker) | n/a |

## The Trainability Certificate

**Export certificate** in the verdict rail writes two files next to your
experiments:

- `experiments/<file-stem>.trainability.json` holds the full results, the
  verdicts and the provenance.
- `experiments/<file-stem>.trainability.md` is a human-readable report: each
  verdict with its evidence chain, every number with its interval and method
  label, the stated assumptions, citations, and a **Reproduce** block.

The provenance records the SHA-256 of your code, the canonical observable, the
settings *as clamped by the kernel*, the versions of Python, NumPy, Qiskit,
Cirq and Stim, a fingerprint of the analysis source, the backend and a
timestamp. Re-running with the same code, settings and seed on the same
backend reproduces every number bit for bit; this is asserted by a kernel test
for the gradient (sampling and exact) and Lie-algebra analyses.

## Honest limits

- **A finite sample is a sample.** A bootstrap interval does not cover
  model error, and variances near 2⁻⁶⁴ cannot be seen by sampling at all. Only
  the exact engine reaches them.
- **Bounded intervals can be loose.** A bounded result is correct but may be
  too wide to decide anything. The Lab shows the width, and a verdict built on
  loose points degrades toward inconclusive.
- **The engine has a class.** Shared parameters, parameter offsets and
  non-Clifford fixed gates push you back to sampling, capped at 20 qubits.
- **The prediction has assumptions.** The Lie-algebraic formula describes deep
  circuits. A shallow circuit can sit far from it, which is why the measured
  scaling outranks the prediction in the verdict.
- **Dimension matching is not identification.** Two different algebras can have
  the same dimension.
- **Scaling compares circuits you build.** If the family you sweep changes
  structure with n, the verdict is about that family, not about your single
  circuit.
- **Fitting needs range.** With fewer than four distinct qubit counts, or a
  window too narrow to separate an exponential from a high-degree polynomial,
  the honest answer is *inconclusive*.
- **Not a training guarantee.** A low risk is not a promise that an optimizer
  will find a good minimum, and a high risk is not a promise it will not. The
  Lab measures gradient concentration, not local minima.
